Frequently asked questions

How is the composite score weighted?

Each of the four criteria (accuracy, latency, VRAM, tokens) is normalised against a target value and multiplied by its weight; the weights are renormalised to sum to 1. The defaults come from the study config. Move the Live weights sliders and the ranking recomputes in your browser with saw.js, the same formula the analysis uses.

Why can every model land in the Critical tier?

Tiers come from fixed thresholds on the composite ×100: Healthy ≥ 80, Degraded ≥ 60, Critical below that. When zero-shot accuracy sits well below the accuracy target, every composite is pulled down, so a whole field can be Critical. That is a finding about zero-shot detection on this data, not a display error.

Does AgentMeter pick the best threat detector?

No. It measures resource efficiency — speed, memory and token use — with accuracy as one factor. The pipeline is the test subject; the contribution is the measurement harness and the comparison method.

What is the difference between total device VRAM and marginal working memory?

Total device VRAM is the memory footprint while the model runs, and it is what the ranking uses. Marginal working memory (vram_delta) is the memory an agent adds above the fresh-context baseline; it excludes the model weights and is shown only in the per-agent diagnostics.

What does the sample banner mean?

The dashboard opens on a bundled sample: accuracy, composite, rank and tier are the locked real results, while latency and token magnitudes and all per-agent numbers are illustrative placeholders. Load a real analysis.json with Load results.json and the banner turns green.

Are runs from the benchmark service comparable with the study?

Service runs use the same pipeline, scoring and 4-bit NF4 settings, but on your data and on whichever GPU the service runs on (named in every result). They are labelled non-validated and never enter this page's locked study or its statistics.

How to read this chart

this is how consistent a model's speed is from scenario to scenario. Lower = steadier, more predictable timing (good for reliable response); a high value means the model is sometimes much slower than its average. The formula is std ÷ mean of end-to-end latency.

Whether the differences between models are real or could be noise.

Statistics are the differences between models real or just noise? (Kruskal-Wallis + Dunn)

How to read these tests

the Kruskal-Wallis test asks whether the models' per-scenario differences are real or could be chance. A small p-value (p < 0.05) means the difference is real, not luck. In the Dunn table below, each cell is the p-value for that pair of models — a small cell means those two genuinely differ.

Glossary

Glossary key terms in plain language

SAW composite score — one 0–1 number blending accuracy, latency, memory and tokens by the chosen weights; higher is better.
Tier — a health label from the score ×100: Healthy (≥ 80), Degraded (≥ 60), Critical (below 60).
Latency — wall-clock time to process one scenario end-to-end through all four agents; lower is better.
TTFT (time to first token) — how long until the model starts producing output — the "prefill" part of a response.
VRAM (working memory) — GPU memory used while running, excluding the model's fixed weights; lower is better.
Tokens — chunks of text the model reads and writes; fewer usually means cheaper and faster.
Zero-shot — the model gets only instructions, no worked examples; it must classify unseen network flows directly.
CoV (coefficient of variation) — spread ÷ average of latency; lower means more consistent, predictable response times.
Pareto front — the models where you can't improve one metric without worsening another — the best available trade-offs.
